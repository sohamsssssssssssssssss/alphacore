## ⚠️ Headline Limitation

**This experiment uses entirely synthetic GBM data, not real market data.**  
Both arms are evaluated on simulated order-book snapshots generated by a geometric Brownian motion model with fixed per-symbol parameters. This data has no real market microstructure (no informed trading, no discrete price levels, no order-book resilience effects). Results may not generalize to real NSE market conditions. Real NSE data validation is explicitly deferred to a later pass.

## The Question (Reframed)

The original question was: "Does NSGA-II-tuned execution beat a naive baseline?" A Step 0 investigation found that this codebase's NSGA-II optimizer (`backend/engines/marl/nsga2_optimizer.py`) tunes **signal combination weights**, not execution parameters (no TWAP/VWAP or child-order scheduling exists in the codebase). The experiment was reframed accordingly:

> **Do NSGA-II-optimized signal-combination weights produce meaningfully different trading outcomes from naive equal weighting, on held-out synthetic data?**

## Sanity Checks

### 1. Lookahead bias ✅
- `_momentum_signal`: uses `snapshots[idx - window]` and `snapshots[idx]` only — no future data
- `_mean_reversion_signal`: uses `snapshots[idx - n + 1]` to `snapshots[idx]` — rolling window, no future
- `_ofi_signal`: uses `snapshots[idx]` only — instant snapshot
- **Confirmed: no lookahead in either arm**

### 2. Degenerate-strategy check ✅
Both arms produced **170 trades each** — well above the 10-trade degeneracy threshold. Neither arm is degenerate.

### 3. Transaction cost consistency ✅
Both arms use the same `CostModel()` instance (identical k_impact, brokerage, STT rate). Costs are applied identically at trade exit. Cost drag is high (>100%) for both, meaning transaction costs exceed gross trading profits — consistent with high-frequency trading on synthetic data with a 0.5% stop-loss and 10-period hold.

### 4. Robustness check 🔶

| Measure | Value | Interpretation |
|---------|-------|---------------|
| Mean diff | ₹-20.11 | Driven by a subset of events |
| Median diff | ₹0.00 | Most snapshots show no difference |
| 5% trimmed mean | ₹-8.70 | About half the raw mean — outliers |
| % of snapshots with diff ≠ 0 | 9.5% (95/1000) | Equity only changes at trade events |
| % negative | 9.3% | Large-negative events dominate |
| % positive | 0.2% | Almost no positive events |

**Interpretation:** The paired difference is driven by a small number of snapshots where trades close at different prices between the two arms. 90.5% of snapshots have zero difference (no trade closed in that period). Among the 9.5% of snapshots with non-zero differences, the large majority favor Arm B (equal weights). The 5% trimmed mean (₹-8.70) is ~43% of the raw mean (₹-20.11), confirming that extreme negative outliers contribute to the mean but the direction is consistent even after trimming.

## Interpretation

**Bottom line: No evidence supports NSGA-II-optimized signal weights over equal weighting on this data. If anything, the evidence weakly favors equal weighting.**

Specifically:

1. **Both arms are unprofitable** on this synthetic data. Raw Sharpe ratios are -2.0 (about -0.29 annualized). Neither strategy generates positive returns after transaction costs. Cost drag exceeds 100% for both.

2. **The paired equity difference is statistically significant** (95% CI excludes zero), but in the **wrong direction**: NSGA-II weights produced slightly worse outcomes (₹-206 on ₹100K capital, or -0.21% return difference).

3. **The Sharpe CIs overlap substantially**, so the Sharpe difference could easily be zero or favor either arm. The annualized Sharps are -0.286 vs -0.260 — both deeply negative and statistically indistinguishable.

4. **The NSGA-II weights put ~54% weight on momentum and ~46% on OFI, with ~0% on mean reversion.** On GBM data with a very weak drift (mu=0.0002), momentum over a short horizon (1-15 snapshots) is essentially noise — past price changes in a GBM have no predictive power for future price changes. Equal weighting dilutes the momentum signal, which happened to help in this case.

5. **This is a clean null/negative result**: the NSGA-II optimizer's selected weights, which appeared optimal on the training sample (seed=42), did not generalize to a new sample from the same data-generating process (seed=2026). This is textbook overfitting to the training set — the optimizer found weights that maximized Sharpe on one GBM path but those weights didn't transfer to another GBM path with identical parameters.

## Limitations

1. **Entirely synthetic data**: GBM data lacks real market microstructure. Both strategies' performance and their relative ranking could change substantially on real NSE data.

2. **Single symbol (RELIANCE)**: Results may not generalize to other symbols with different liquidity, volatility, or micro-structure.

3. **Single regime**: The GBM parameters (sigma=0.20, mu=0.0002) represent one specific volatility/drift regime. Different market conditions could produce different relative rankings.

4. **No execution layer**: Both arms use the identical execution path (immediate fill at bid/ask). The question of whether NSGA-II can optimize **execution parameters** (participation rate, aggressiveness, child-order scheduling) remains unanswered — that requires a proper execution layer that doesn't exist in this codebase.

5. **Modest statistical power**: Even with 1,000 snapshots, only ~95 snapshots (9.5%) have non-zero equity differences. The effective sample size for the paired comparison is closer to 95 than 1,000. The block bootstrap accounts for this via autocorrelation-respecting resampling, but wider data would strengthen confidence.

6. **Pre-committed weights**: The NSGA-II weights were locked on a previous training run (seed=42, 866 snapshots) and applied to a new sample (seed=2026). A different training sample or a different NSGA-II configuration (more generations, different population size) could produce different weights. The result reported here is specific to these particular weights.

---

## Conclusion

**The data does not support the claim that NSGA-II-optimized signal weights beat equal weighting on this synthetic data.** The opposite appears to be true: equal weighting produced slightly better outcomes, and NSGA-II weights marginally underperformed. However, both arms lose money — the more important finding is that on GBM-simulated data with realistic costs, neither simple equal-weighted signal combination nor NSGA-II-optimized signal combination generates positive returns.

This is a **null/negative result**, honestly reported. A meaningful answer to the original "execution" question would require:
1. Building an actual execution layer (TWAP/VWAP scheduler) with tunable parameters
2. Using NSGA-II to optimize those execution parameters
3. Evaluating on real NSE historical tick data
